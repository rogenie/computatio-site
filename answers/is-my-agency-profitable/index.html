How do I know if my agency is actually profitable?

Your agency is actually profitable when a reconciled profit and loss report shows money left over after you subtract the direct cost of delivering client work, every overhead expense and a fair wage for the time you put in. If profit only appears because you paid yourself nothing, or because the books are missing transactions, the agency is not yet profitable in a way you can rely on.

By Computatio · Reviewed by Rogenie Cordero, founder · Updated November 24, 2026

Why can revenue look fine while cash is tight?

Revenue, profit and cash measure different things. The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) describes a profit and loss statement as a report that measures net income or loss over a defined period of time. Cash is simply what is in the bank today.

The gap usually comes from timing. The IRS explains that under the accrual method you generally report income in the tax year you earn it, regardless of when payment is received. So an agency that invoiced $40,000 in October can show a strong October profit while the money arrives in December. Contractors, payroll and software subscriptions still leave the bank on schedule.

QuickBooks Online lets you see both views. Intuit says a cash-basis report counts income or expenses only once you get a payment or pay a bill, while an accrual-basis report counts them whether or not the invoice or bill was paid. Run the same month both ways and compare.

What's the difference between gross profit and net profit?

Gross profit tells you whether client work pays for itself. Net profit tells you whether the whole business does. The SBA defines gross profit as income less cost of goods sold, and cost of goods sold as the direct cost of producing your product or service. For an agency, that usually means contractor and freelancer costs, wages for the people doing billable work, and any costs you pass through on a client's behalf.

Net income is what is left after all expenses: rent, software, insurance, sales and admin time, and taxes. Use the SBA's two ratios to compare months of different sizes:

MeasureFormulaWhat it answers
Gross profitRevenue − direct delivery costsDoes client work cover the people and tools that deliver it?
Gross profit margin(Revenue − direct costs) ÷ revenueHow much of each dollar of fees is left after delivery?
Net profitRevenue − all expensesDoes the business make money after overhead?
Profit margin(Revenue − all expenses) ÷ revenueHow much of each dollar of fees the business keeps
Profit after owner payNet profit − a fair wage for your hoursWould the agency still make money if you hired someone to do your job?

These only work if direct costs are in their own section of the chart of accounts. If contractors sit in the same bucket as office supplies, gross profit can't be measured.

How should I count my own pay?

Owner pay is where agency profit is most often overstated. If you are a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, the money you take out is usually recorded as an owner draw, which does not appear on the profit and loss report. A report that shows $8,000 of monthly profit may be the only thing paying you.

To test real profitability, subtract what it would cost to pay someone else for the work you do. If that leaves nothing, the agency is a job that pays you, not yet a business that earns a profit on top of your wage.

If your agency is an S corporation, the IRS says it must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee for services before non-wage distributions may be made. The IRS lists factors such as duties and responsibilities, time and effort devoted to the business, and what comparable businesses pay for similar services. Ask your tax preparer what fits your situation.

How do I read my profit and loss report?

Read the report in the same order every month. In QuickBooks Online, go to Reports, then Standard reports, and choose Profit and Loss Comparison. Intuit's help page says you can set the accounting method to Cash or Accrual and compare to the previous period or the previous year, with a dollar or percent change.

  • Revenue: is it from a few large clients or many? Is any of it a pass-through cost you'll pay out again?
  • Direct costs: did they rise faster than revenue?
  • Gross profit margin: is it steady month to month?
  • Overhead: which subscriptions or fees grew without anyone deciding they should?
  • Net profit: does it hold up after a fair owner wage?

None of this is reliable until every account is reconciled. If you're months behind, start with how to catch up on months of bookkeeping.

Example: a 6-person design studio

All of these are made-up numbers to show the math, not results from a real client.

  • October revenue: $60,000 invoiced, of which $5,000 is printing passed through to a client (made-up numbers).
  • Direct costs: $22,000 for the designers and freelancers on client work, plus the $5,000 printing (made-up numbers). Gross profit: $60,000 − $27,000 = $33,000, a 55% gross margin. Without the pass-through, fee revenue is $55,000 and fee gross margin is $33,000 ÷ $55,000 = 60% (made-up numbers).
  • Overhead: $21,000 for rent, software, insurance, an office manager and marketing (made-up numbers). Net profit: $33,000 − $21,000 = $12,000 (made-up numbers).
  • The owner takes no salary and draws $10,000 a month. Paying someone else to run the studio and lead creative would cost about $11,000 a month (made-up numbers). Profit after owner pay: $12,000 − $11,000 = $1,000 (made-up numbers).
  • Cash: $35,000 of October invoices won't be paid until December, so the bank balance falls even though October was profitable (made-up numbers).

The studio is profitable, but barely, once the owner's work is counted. Its cash problem is collections timing, not a lack of profit.

What should I compare my numbers against?

Compare your agency with itself first. The SBA suggests comparing ratios over three years to find trends and says industry standards are a reference, not a recipe, because what's healthy depends on your company and industry. We don't quote agency benchmark ranges here because we haven't found an official source for them.

Track gross margin and profit after owner pay monthly, and by client if you can. A falling gross margin often means scope creep or underpriced retainers. Flat revenue with rising overhead points to costs that grew without a decision.

FAQ

Is profit the same as cash in the bank?
No. Profit measures income minus expenses for a period, while cash depends on when clients pay and when you pay bills. Run your profit and loss report on both a cash and an accrual basis to see the gap.
Do owner draws show up on the profit and loss report?
Usually not. For sole proprietors and single-member LLCs, draws are typically recorded in equity, so the report can show a profit that is really your pay.
What counts as a direct cost for an agency?
The costs of delivering client work: contractors, freelancers, wages for billable staff and anything you buy on a client's behalf. Keep them in their own section of the chart of accounts so gross profit is visible.
Should I use cash or accrual reports?
Look at both. Accrual shows what you earned in a month; cash shows what actually came in and went out. The method on your tax return is a separate choice; ask your tax preparer which one your business uses.
How often should I check profitability?
Every month, after all accounts are reconciled. Compare each month with the previous month and the same month last year.
